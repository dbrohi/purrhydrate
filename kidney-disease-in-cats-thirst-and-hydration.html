Kidney Disease in Cats: Thirst, Hydration and Warning Signs

Published: October 19, 2026

Chronic kidney disease is one of the most common health problems in older cats, and changes in thirst are often one of the first things owners notice. This guide explains how the kidneys relate to water, which signs to watch for, and how hydration fits into care. It cannot diagnose your cat, and only a vet can do that.

How the kidneys handle water

The kidneys filter waste from the blood and decide how much water to keep and how much to pass as urine. Healthy kidneys make concentrated urine, which saves water. When kidney function declines, the kidneys can no longer concentrate urine as well, so the cat passes larger amounts of more dilute urine. To make up for the lost fluid, the cat drinks more. Even so, a cat with kidney disease can still become dehydrated, and dehydration can put more strain on the kidneys.

Warning signs to watch for

Kidney disease usually develops slowly, so the early signs are easy to miss or to put down to ageing:

  • Drinking more than usual, or emptying the water bowl faster
  • Larger or more frequent urine clumps in the litter box
  • Weight loss, often with loss of muscle
  • Reduced appetite or fussiness with food
  • Vomiting, nausea or drooling
  • Bad breath, mouth ulcers, or a dull, unkempt coat
  • Tiredness, constipation or hiding

None of these signs proves kidney disease, since other conditions such as diabetes and an overactive thyroid cause similar changes. That is why a vet visit matters.

If your cat is drinking much more than usual, book a vet check. Do not wait to see whether it passes, and do not limit the water. Early detection makes the condition much easier to manage.

How a vet checks for kidney disease

A vet will usually examine your cat and run blood tests to look at kidney values, test the urine to see how concentrated it is, and often check blood pressure. These tests can also pick up other causes of increased thirst. If kidney disease is found, the vet can stage it and suggest a plan.

Stages of kidney disease

Vets often describe chronic kidney disease in stages, usually numbered from 1 to 4, based on blood test results and urine tests. Early stages may cause few or no obvious signs, which is why routine check-ups for older cats are so useful. Later stages usually bring clearer signs such as weight loss, vomiting and poor appetite. The stage helps your vet decide on diet, treatment and how often to recheck your cat. It does not by itself say how long a cat will live, since many cats stay stable for a long time.

Where hydration fits into care

Kidney disease cannot usually be cured, but many cats live comfortably for a long time with good management. Hydration is a central part of that care. Your vet may suggest one or more of the following:

  • A kidney-friendly prescription diet, which is often available as moist food
  • More moisture in the diet, such as wet food, since moist food adds water without needing the cat to drink
  • Fluid therapy, for some cats, given under the skin at home after the vet has taught you how, or at the clinic
  • Treatment of related problems, such as high blood pressure or nausea

Do not start fluids or change a prescription diet on your own. The right plan depends on the stage of the disease and on your cat.

What you can do at home

  1. Keep fresh water available at all times, in more than one place.
  2. Never restrict water, even if your cat drinks a lot.
  3. Use a wide, shallow bowl, and consider a fountain if your cat likes running water.
  4. Ask your vet whether more wet food is right for your cat. See wet vs dry food for hydration.
  5. Watch the litter box, and note changes in clump size, appetite and weight.
  6. Keep regular check-ups, as older cats benefit from routine blood and urine tests.

For more ideas on encouraging drinking, see how to hydrate a cat at home.

Monitoring your cat at home

If your cat has been diagnosed with kidney disease, your vet will often ask you to watch a few things at home. Weigh your cat regularly and keep a note, because weight loss can be an early sign that things are changing. Notice appetite, since eating less is a common warning. Keep an eye on the litter box and on how fast the water bowl empties. Give any medication exactly as prescribed. Bring your notes to rechecks, because small changes seen over weeks can help your vet adjust the plan. If your cat suddenly stops eating, vomits repeatedly, or seems weak, call your vet the same day.

Quick questions

Can kidney disease in cats be cured? Usually not, but it can often be managed, and many cats do well for years with the right care.

Does drinking a lot of water cause kidney disease? No. Increased drinking is usually a result of kidney disease, not a cause.

Is wet food better for a cat with kidney disease? Vets often recommend moist food, because it adds water. Ask your vet which food suits your cat.

How can I check how much my cat should drink? The calculator gives a typical range for healthy cats. A cat with kidney disease may need different amounts, so follow your vet's advice.
