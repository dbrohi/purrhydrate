When to Call the Vet About Your Cat's Water Intake

Published: October 31, 2026

Most cats that drink a little less than usual are fine, especially cats that eat wet food. Changes in drinking can also be an early sign of illness, though. This guide sorts the signs into three groups so you know whether to watch, call, or go now.

Go to an emergency vet now if your cat is weak or collapsed, has pale, white or blue gums, is vomiting repeatedly, or is straining to urinate with little or no urine (especially male cats). Straining can mean a blocked bladder, which can be life-threatening within a day.

Quick guide

What you seeWhat to doHow soon
Dry or sticky gums, slow skin return, or not eating and drinking for a dayCall your vetThe same day
Vomiting or diarrhea that lasts more than a dayCall your vetThe same day
Drinking much more than usual, or much less, for several daysBook a check-upWithin a few days
Drinking a little less, but eating, playing and using the litter box normallyOffer fresh water and wet food, then watchRe-check in 24 hours

The table is a general guide. If you are unsure, calling your vet is always reasonable.

Drinking more than usual

A cat that suddenly drinks much more can have a health problem such as kidney disease, diabetes or an overactive thyroid. This is not something to wait out. Note how much your cat drinks and how often it uses the litter box, then see why a cat may drink so much water and book a visit.

Drinking less than usual

Cats that eat wet food often drink very little from the bowl, and that can be normal. A cat that is drinking less and also eating less, hiding or looking tired is different. Cornell Feline Health Center lists lethargy, weakness, poor appetite, dry gums and sunken eyes among the signs of dehydration. For simple ways to encourage drinking, see how to hydrate a cat at home.

Cats that need earlier help

Call sooner rather than later for kittens, senior cats, and cats with kidney disease, diabetes or hyperthyroidism, because they can become dehydrated more easily.

What to tell the vet

  • How long the change has lasted
  • Eating, drinking and litter box habits, including any change in urine clumps
  • Any vomiting, diarrhea, new medicine or new food
  • Your cat's age and any known conditions

Nights and weekends

If your regular clinic is closed, ask for its out-of-hours number, or find the nearest emergency vet. Phoning ahead lets them prepare and tell you whether to come in. There is more in what to do if you suspect dehydration at night or on a weekend.
