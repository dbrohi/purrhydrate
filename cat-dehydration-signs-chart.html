Cat Dehydration Chart: Signs, Simple Checks and When to See a Vet

Published: October 8, 2026

Dehydration in cats can be hard to spot early, because cats hide illness well. This guide lists the signs, explains the common at-home checks and their limits, and says when to get help. It cannot replace an exam by a vet.

What does a dehydrated cat look like?

A dehydrated cat often looks tired and quiet, may have dry or sticky gums and slightly sunken eyes, and may eat less than usual. The skin may be slow to settle back into place when gently lifted, and the cat may pass less urine. In the early stages there may be few visible signs at all, so a cat can look almost normal while already low on fluid.

How do you tell if a cat is dehydrated?

Check four things: the gums, the skin, the litter box, and your cat's energy and appetite. Healthy gums are pink and moist, and the skin between the shoulder blades should fall back quickly when lifted. Fewer or smaller urine clumps, tiredness or refusing food add to the picture. If the gums feel dry or sticky, the skin is slow to return, or your cat is weak, call your vet. The step-by-step checks are explained below.

Why dehydration is easy to miss

Cats are good at hiding discomfort, and the early signs of dehydration are subtle. A mildly dehydrated cat may still walk, eat and act normally, so many owners notice only when the problem has become more serious. That is why it helps to know your cat's usual habits, such as how often it uses the litter box, how much it drinks, and how it behaves, so that you can spot a change early.

Common signs of dehydration

  • Dry, sticky or tacky gums (healthy gums are moist and pink)
  • Lethargy or unusual weakness
  • Loss of appetite
  • Sunken eyes
  • Skin that is slow to return to place when gently lifted
  • Less urine, or dark, strong-smelling urine
  • Constipation
  • Panting or a fast heartbeat in more serious cases

Rough guide to levels of dehydration

Vets describe dehydration as a percentage of body weight lost as fluid. Owners cannot measure this at home, so use the table only as an idea of how signs build up.

LevelWhat may be seen
Mild (about 5%)Often no obvious signs, or slightly dry gums.
Moderate (about 6–8%)Skin slow to return, dry or tacky gums, tiredness, slightly sunken eyes.
Severe (10% or more)Skin stays tented, very dry gums, sunken eyes, weakness, cold legs, fast heartbeat. This is an emergency.

Simple checks and their limits

  • Gum check: lift the lip. Gums should look moist and pink. Dry or tacky gums are a warning sign. Pale, white or blue gums need urgent care.
  • Skin test: gently lift the skin between the shoulder blades and let go. It should snap back quickly. This test is unreliable in overweight, very thin and older cats, so do not rely on it alone.
  • Litter box: fewer or smaller urine clumps than usual can mean low fluid intake or a health problem.
Get urgent veterinary help if your cat is weak, collapsed, has pale gums, is vomiting repeatedly, has diarrhea, or strains to urinate with little or no urine (especially male cats). Straining can mean a blocked bladder, which can be life-threatening within a day.

Common reasons for dehydration

Vomiting, diarrhea, fever, hot weather, kidney disease, diabetes, and simply drinking too little on a dry-food diet. Many cats that seem "lazy drinkers" are fine. A sudden change from your cat's normal habits is the signal to act.

A quick check you can do at home, step by step

  1. Watch first. Is your cat alert, moving normally, and interested in food? Hiding, listlessness or refusing food is a warning on its own.
  2. Check the gums. Gently lift the lip. They should be pink and wet. If they feel dry, sticky, or look pale, note it.
  3. Check capillary refill. Press a finger on the gum until the spot turns pale, then let go. The pink color should come back in about one to two seconds. A slower return can be a sign of dehydration or poor circulation, and needs a vet.
  4. Try the skin test. Lift a small fold of skin between the shoulder blades and release. It should fall back at once.
  5. Look at the litter box. Fewer, smaller or darker urine clumps than usual is worth noting.

Do not try these checks if your cat is stressed or aggressive, and do not use them to decide that your cat is "fine." A normal result does not rule out a problem.

Cats at higher risk

  • Kittens and senior cats
  • Cats with kidney disease, diabetes or hyperthyroidism
  • Cats with vomiting, diarrhea or a fever
  • Cats recovering from surgery or illness
  • Cats eating only dry food, especially in hot weather

If your cat is in one of these groups, call your vet sooner rather than later when something seems off.

What a vet will do

A vet will examine your cat, check the gums, skin and heart rate, and may run blood and urine tests to find the cause. Treatment usually means giving fluids, either under the skin or through a vein, and treating whatever caused the dehydration. Fluids should be given at home only if a vet has prescribed them and shown you how. Giving fluids incorrectly can be dangerous.

What to do next

If you only suspect your cat is not drinking enough and there are no warning signs, you can try safe ways to encourage drinking. To see how much your cat needs, use the calculator or read how much water a cat should drink. If in doubt, call your vet.

Quick questions

My cat eats wet food and rarely drinks. Is that dehydration? Not necessarily. A cat on wet food gets much of its water from meals and may drink little. Look at the other signs, such as gums, energy and litter box habits.

Can a cat recover from mild dehydration at home? If your cat is bright, eating and has no other symptoms, encouraging drinking may be enough, and our guide to hydrating a cat at home explains how. If you are unsure, or your cat does not improve quickly, ask your vet.

How long is too long for a cat to go without drinking? There is no exact rule, since it depends on the cat's diet and health. As a general guide, a cat that is not eating or drinking for a day, or is also vomiting or lethargic, should be seen by a vet.
