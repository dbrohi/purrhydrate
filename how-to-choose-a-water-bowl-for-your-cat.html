How to Choose a Water Bowl for Your Cat: Size, Material and Placement

Published: October 19, 2026

The right water bowl can make a real difference to how much your cat drinks. Cats are fussy, and a bowl that is too deep, too small, or in the wrong place may put a cat off. This guide explains what to look for in size, shape and material, where to put the bowl, and how to keep it clean.

Why the bowl matters

Cats that eat dry food need to drink most of their water, so a bowl they like is important. Even cats on wet food should always have fresh water available. Small details, such as whether the whiskers touch the sides or whether the water smells stale, can change how often a cat drinks.

Size and shape

  • Wide: choose a bowl wide enough for your cat's face and whiskers to fit without touching the sides. As a rough guide, many owners look for around 5–6 inches (13–15 cm) across, bigger for large cats.
  • Shallow: a low rim is easier for most cats, especially kittens and seniors.
  • Stable: a heavy or non-slip base stops the bowl sliding or tipping.
  • Easy to clean: smooth surfaces and no tight corners are easier to wash thoroughly.

Some cats seem bothered when their whiskers touch the bowl. Our bowl check shows a simple way to test this.

Where to put the bowl

  1. A quiet place: away from loud appliances, busy walkways and the litter box.
  2. Not right next to the food: many cats seem to prefer their water kept separate from their food, so try moving it if your cat ignores the bowl.
  3. More than one spot: two or three bowls around the home, especially where your cat rests, make drinking easier.
  4. Easy access: a senior or stiff cat may need bowls on each floor.

How many bowls do you need?

For one cat, two or three bowls in different places is a good start. In a home with several cats, give each cat its own access, and add a few extra, since some cats avoid drinking near another cat. See our guide to fountains if you would like to try moving water.

Keeping the bowl clean

  • Change the water and wash the bowl every day.
  • Use plain, unscented dish soap and rinse very well, because strong smells can put a cat off.
  • Replace plastic or ceramic bowls that are scratched or chipped.
  • Do a deeper clean once a week, since a thin slimy layer called biofilm builds up on bowls.

Testing a new bowl

Cats do not always accept a new bowl at once. Put the new bowl next to the old one for a few days, so your cat can compare, and fill both with fresh water. Watch which one your cat uses. If the new bowl is ignored, try another spot rather than assuming the bowl is wrong. Change only one thing at a time, such as the bowl or the location, so you can tell what helped.

Kittens, seniors and multi-cat homes

  • Kittens: a shallow, stable dish that cannot tip is safest, and they may need it nearer to where they play.
  • Senior or stiff cats: low-sided bowls and easy access on every floor matter most, and some older cats do better with a slightly raised bowl.
  • Several cats: put bowls in separate places so that a shy cat can drink without meeting a bolder one.

For more on very young cats, see how much water a kitten should drink.

When to replace a bowl

Bowls do not last forever. Replace a bowl when it is scratched, chipped, cracked or stained, because damaged surfaces are harder to clean and can hold bacteria. Plastic bowls tend to wear out fastest. If your cat has developed small bumps or blackheads on its chin, ask your vet whether the bowl could be a factor, and try switching to stainless steel, glass or ceramic. A good bowl is a small, low-cost way to support your cat's health, so it is worth checking the condition of every bowl in your home from time to time.

Common mistakes

  • A deep, narrow bowl that touches the whiskers
  • A single bowl in one corner
  • Topping up old water instead of replacing it
  • Placing water next to the litter box
  • Strongly scented soap left on the bowl

Quick questions

Do cats prefer a full bowl or a half-full one? It varies. Some seem to like a bowl filled to the top, so they do not have to reach down. Try both and see.

Is a raised bowl better? Some older cats find a slightly raised bowl easier, but evidence is mixed, so watch what your own cat prefers.

My cat still does not drink. What now? Try the ideas in how to hydrate a cat at home. If your cat drinks much less than usual or seems unwell, see why a cat may not drink and call your vet.

This article is general information, not veterinary advice or a diagnosis. If you are worried about your cat, contact your vet.
