Why Isn't My Cat Drinking Water? Causes and What to Do

Published: October 9, 2026

It is worrying when you never see your cat at the water bowl. The good news is that it is often normal, especially for cats that eat wet food. This guide explains the most common reasons, what to try, and when it is a sign of something more serious.

First, is it really a problem?

Start with your cat's food. Wet food is about 75–80% water, so a cat that eats mostly wet food can drink very little from the bowl and still be well hydrated. A cat on dry food, which is only about 10% water, has to get nearly all of its water by drinking. If your cat eats dry food and you truly never see it drink, that deserves attention. If it eats wet food, is bright and active, and uses the litter box normally, there is usually nothing to worry about. You can check what is typical for your cat with the calculator.

Common everyday reasons

  • Diet: as above, wet food reduces the need to drink.
  • The bowl: some cats dislike deep or narrow bowls that touch their whiskers, or plastic that holds odors. See our bowl check.
  • Stale or dirty water: cats can be picky. Fresh water and a clean bowl every day make a difference.
  • Location: a bowl placed right next to the food or the litter box may be ignored. Many cats prefer water in a quiet spot, apart from their food.
  • Water preference: some cats like moving water, a glass, or the tap, and ignore a still bowl.
  • Stress and change: a move, a new pet or baby, or loud noise can change eating and drinking habits for a while.

Health reasons that reduce drinking

Pain or illness can make a cat drink less. Dental disease can make drinking uncomfortable, nausea can put a cat off food and water, and arthritis can make it hard to reach a bowl, especially if it is raised or on another floor. Cats that are unwell often become quiet and may hide. Reduced drinking together with these changes is not something to wait out.

Note: the opposite problem matters too. A cat that suddenly drinks much more than usual should also be checked by a vet, as it can be linked to kidney disease, diabetes or an overactive thyroid.

What to try at home

  1. Wash the bowl and give fresh water daily.
  2. Switch to a wide, shallow bowl made of glass, ceramic or stainless steel.
  3. Offer water in two or three quiet places around the home.
  4. Add a spoonful of warm water or a little wet food to a meal.
  5. Try a fountain if your cat likes running water.

Our guide to hydrating a cat at home explains each of these in more detail, including what not to do, such as forcing water with a syringe.

Kittens and senior cats

Age changes the picture. Kittens are small and can become dehydrated faster than adults, so a kitten that is not drinking, or not eating, should be taken seriously sooner. Senior cats are more likely to have dental pain, arthritis, or kidney problems, all of which can affect drinking. An older cat that has quietly cut back on water may simply be finding the bowl harder to reach, so try a lower bowl on each floor. If a senior cat's habits change and you cannot find an easy reason, mention it to your vet at the next visit, or sooner if there are other signs.

Seasons and routine

Many cats drink a little less in cool weather and a little more in summer. Small changes like this are normal. Changes in routine, such as new work hours, guests, or rearranged furniture, can also affect habits for a few days. What matters is a lasting change from your own cat's usual pattern, not a single quiet day.

Keep a simple record

If you are unsure whether your cat is drinking enough, write down a few things for a few days: what and how much it eats, whether the bowl level changes, how many urine clumps are in the litter box, and how it behaves. The tracker on the homepage is a simple place to do this. Notes like these make it much easier to tell a normal quirk from a real change, and they help your vet if you do need an appointment.

When to call the vet

  • Your cat is not eating, or is eating and drinking much less than normal for more than a day
  • Lethargy, hiding, vomiting or diarrhea
  • Dry or sticky gums, or sunken eyes (see the dehydration chart)
  • Straining to urinate, or passing little or no urine, which can be an emergency, especially in male cats
  • Kittens, senior cats, or cats with a known health condition that stop drinking

What your vet may ask

If you do call the vet, it helps to have answers ready. They will often ask what your cat eats and how much, how long the change has lasted, whether appetite, energy or weight have changed, how the litter box looks, whether there is vomiting or diarrhea, and whether anything has changed at home. Bring your notes if you made any.

Quick questions

Why does my cat drink from the tap or a glass but not the bowl? It is usually a preference for fresh, moving or elevated water, not a problem. A fountain or a fresh bowl may satisfy it.

My cat eats wet food and never drinks. Should I worry? Not by itself. Look at the other signs: energy, gums, appetite and litter box habits.

How much should my cat drink? It depends on weight and diet. See how much water a cat should drink.

This article is general information, not veterinary advice. If you are worried about your cat, contact your vet.
