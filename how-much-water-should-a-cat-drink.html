How Much Water Should a Cat Drink Per Day?

Published: October 8, 2026

Cat owners usually ask two questions: how much water should my cat drink, and how often? There is no fixed schedule for drinking. What matters is the total amount of water your cat gets across the whole day, from the bowl and from food.

The short answer

A commonly cited guideline is about 50–60 mL of total water per kilogram of body weight per day (roughly 0.8–0.9 fl oz per pound). "Total" means everything: water from the bowl plus the moisture in your cat's food.

Cat's weightTotal water per day (typical range)
6 lb (2.7 kg)136–163 mL (4.6–5.5 fl oz)
8 lb (3.6 kg)181–218 mL (6.1–7.4 fl oz)
10 lb (4.5 kg)227–272 mL (7.7–9.2 fl oz)
12 lb (5.4 kg)272–327 mL (9.2–11.0 fl oz)
15 lb (6.8 kg)340–408 mL (11.5–13.8 fl oz)

These are rules of thumb for a healthy adult cat. Individual cats vary, so treat them as a guide, not a target to hit exactly.

Why your cat may drink less than the chart says

Food counts. Wet (canned) food is roughly 75–80% water, while dry food is only about 10%. A cat eating mostly wet food can get a large share of its daily water from meals and may barely touch the bowl. A cat eating only dry food has to drink much more to reach the same total. That is why one generic number, such as "a cup a day," often does not match what a real cat does.

How often should a cat drink?

There is no correct number of drinks. Most cats take several small sips spread through the day and night. Instead of counting visits to the bowl, watch for changes: a cat that suddenly drinks much more or much less than usual is worth noticing.

What changes how much a cat needs

  • Diet: dry food means more drinking, wet food means less.
  • Weather and activity: hot weather and active play increase needs.
  • Age: kittens and senior cats can have different needs.
  • Health: illness, pregnancy and nursing all change how much water a cat needs.
Drinking much more than usual is a reason to call your vet. Increased thirst can be an early sign of conditions such as kidney disease, diabetes or an overactive thyroid. Do not assume it is just the weather. See why a cat may be drinking so much.

Two worked examples: why diet changes the answer

Take a healthy 10 lb (4.5 kg) cat. Its total daily water need is about 227–272 mL. How much of that has to come from the bowl depends on what is in the food dish.

  • Dry food only: a cat eating about 50 g of kibble gets only around 5 mL of water from it. Nearly all of the 227–272 mL has to be drunk, which is roughly 7.5–9 fl oz from the bowl.
  • Wet food only: two 3 oz (85 g) cans are about 170 g of food, and at roughly 78% moisture that is around 133 mL of water. The cat only needs to drink about 95–140 mL, or 3–5 fl oz, to reach the same total.

These are rough estimates, since food types and portion sizes vary. They show why two healthy cats of the same weight can look completely different at the water bowl. The PurrHydrate calculator uses simplified versions of these assumptions to split your cat's total between the bowl and food.

Kittens, seniors and cats with health conditions

Kittens are growing quickly and tend to need more water for their size, and they are more vulnerable if they become dehydrated. Senior cats are more likely to have kidney disease, diabetes or an overactive thyroid, all of which can change thirst. For these cats, the guideline above is only a starting point, and your vet's advice comes first. Cats on prescription diets, such as kidney diets, may also have specific moisture targets set by their vet.

Can you measure how much your cat drinks?

You can get a rough idea, but it is harder than it sounds. One simple method is to measure the water you pour into the bowl, then measure what is left 24 hours later. The difference is approximately what was drunk, though some water evaporates or gets splashed. In a home with more than one cat, or with a fountain or several bowls, this does not work well, because you cannot tell who drank what.

For most owners, there is no need to measure every day. It is more useful to notice changes: a bowl that is suddenly emptied much faster than usual, or one that barely changes for several days in a cat on dry food. If you are worried about a change, write it down and tell your vet.

Common myths about cat water

  • "Cats get all the water they need from their food." This is true only for cats eating mostly wet food. Cats on dry food need to drink much more.
  • "Cats need milk." Adult cats do not need milk, and many are lactose intolerant. Plain, fresh water is what counts.
  • "A cat that rarely drinks is always fine." Often true on wet food, but not on dry food. If your cat eats dry food and you never see it drink, it is worth looking into.

How to tell if your cat is getting enough

You cannot judge this by watching the bowl alone. Better signs are normal-sized urine clumps in the litter box, moist gums, steady energy and a normal appetite. If you are unsure, read our guide to signs of dehydration in cats.

Quick questions

Is a cup of water a day enough? It depends on the cat. A cup is about 8 fl oz (237 mL), which is close to the total need of a 9 lb cat, but only if that cup is all the water the cat gets. A cat on wet food gets part of its water from meals, and a larger cat needs more.

Should I worry if my cat drinks from the tap or a glass instead of the bowl? Usually not. Many cats prefer fresh, moving or elevated water. It is a preference, not a problem.

Is it a problem if my cat drinks more in summer? A small rise in hot weather is normal. A big, lasting increase, or increased drinking with weight loss or more urination, should be checked by a vet.

This article is general information, not veterinary advice. Please talk to your vet about your cat's specific needs.
