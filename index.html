Signs your cat may be dehydrated

A cat that's quietly under-drinking rarely makes it obvious. These are the physical signs worth checking for, especially if your cat eats mostly dry food.

Loss of skin elasticity Gently lift the skin between the shoulder blades. In a well-hydrated cat it snaps back quickly — in a dehydrated one, it settles back slowly or stays "tented."
Dry or tacky gums Healthy gums feel wet and slick to the touch. Dry, tacky, or unusually pale gums are one of the more reliable early signs of fluid loss.
Sunken eyes The eyes can look noticeably sunken or lose their usual fullness. This is more common in moderate-to-severe dehydration than in mild cases.
Lethargy or low energy A dehydrated cat often sleeps more than usual, seems unusually withdrawn, or is reluctant to jump, play, or move around.
Reduced appetite Dehydration and reduced eating often show up together — a cat that skips meals for more than a day is worth watching closely for other signs too.
Thick or stringy saliva Saliva that looks unusually thick, stringy, or foamy can accompany more advanced dehydration, often alongside panting or excessive grooming around the mouth.
Call your vet immediately for severe symptoms Repeated vomiting or diarrhea, collapse, extreme lethargy, a skin tent that doesn't spring back at all, or refusal to eat or drink for over 24 hours can point to serious or rapidly worsening dehydration. Don't wait to see if it improves on its own — contact your veterinarian or an emergency animal hospital right away.

When water changes point to something more than thirst

This calculator gives a general target — it can't tell the difference between a hot day and a disease process. Several common feline conditions change how much a cat drinks, sometimes dramatically, and catching that shift early matters.

Chronic Kidney Disease

CKD
↑ Usually increases thirst

As kidney function declines, the kidneys lose their ability to concentrate urine, so the body loses more water than normal and compensates by drinking more. It's most common in cats over 10, and increased thirst is often one of the earliest visible signs — sometimes appearing before a cat seems sick in any other way.

Watch for:
  • Noticeably increased drinking and urination
  • Weight loss despite normal or increased appetite
  • Larger, more frequent clumps in the litter box

Feline Lower Urinary Tract Disease

FLUTD
⚠ Often reduces drinking

FLUTD is an umbrella term for bladder inflammation, crystals, or stones in the urinary tract. Cats often drink less because urinating has become painful, which sets up a dangerous cycle — less water intake means more concentrated urine, which worsens irritation and crystal formation.

Watch for:
  • Straining, crying, or frequent trips to the litter box with little urine produced
  • Blood-tinged urine or urinating outside the litter box
  • In male cats especially: a fully blocked urethra is a medical emergency

Diabetes Mellitus

Diabetes
↑ Usually increases thirst

When blood sugar runs too high, excess glucose spills into the urine and pulls water along with it. That extra fluid loss triggers a big compensatory increase in thirst — often one of the first things an owner notices, sometimes alongside a bigger appetite paired with unexplained weight loss.

Watch for:
  • Marked increase in drinking and urination
  • Increased appetite with weight loss
  • Unusual weakness or a wobbly, flat-footed walk
Why a sudden change in drinking needs a vet, not just a watchful eye

A hydration calculator — or any home checklist — can't distinguish a hot afternoon or a diet change from CKD, diabetes, hyperthyroidism, liver disease, or a urinary blockage. Several of these look almost identical from the outside: more thirst, more urination, maybe some weight change. The only way to tell them apart is bloodwork and a urinalysis, which is exactly what a vet visit is for.

This matters because early detection changes outcomes. CKD and diabetes are both far easier to manage — and slow down — when caught early, before there's lasting organ damage. If your cat's drinking noticeably more or less than usual for more than a day or two, or you've noticed any of the warning signs above, that's a reason to call your vet, not to wait and monitor further.

Practical ways to get your cat drinking more

Cats are picky about water for real, physical reasons. Small changes to placement, bowl choice, and freshness usually work better than nagging your cat to drink.

Keep water away from litter boxes and food In the wild, cats instinctively avoid drinking near where they eliminate or eat, since it signals contaminated water. Placing bowls in a separate, quiet spot can make a real difference.
Choose wide, shallow bowls Deep, narrow bowls press against a cat's sensitive whiskers with every sip — known as "whisker fatigue." Wide ceramic or stainless steel bowls let them drink without that pressure.
Offer plain, low-sodium broth ice cubes Freeze a small amount of plain, low-sodium chicken or fish broth (no onion, garlic, or seasoning) into cubes. The scent and flavor can tempt cats who ignore a plain water bowl.
Set up more than one water station Cats in multi-level or multi-room homes often drink more when water is available in two or three spots, rather than a single bowl they have to seek out.
Wash and refill bowls daily A film builds up on standing water within a day, changing its taste and smell in ways cats notice immediately. Fresh water in a clean bowl gets used far more reliably.
Add moisture through food Mixing in wet food, or adding a splash of water or low-sodium broth to dry kibble, is one of the most effective ways to raise total water intake without changing drinking habits at all.

Frequently asked questions

The questions we hear most from cat owners trying to get their cat to drink more.

Why does my cat prefer running tap water?

It's largely instinct. In the wild, still water is more likely to be stagnant or contaminated, while moving water — a stream, a puddle being stirred by wind — usually means it's fresher and safer to drink. Cats carry that same instinct around a still bowl versus a running tap or fountain.

It's also partly sensory: running water agitates and aerates, which can make it taste and smell different (often preferred) compared to water that's been sitting still, especially if it's picked up any odor from the bowl itself.

Is tap water safe, or should I use filtered water?

For most cats on a typical municipal water supply, tap water is perfectly safe. The main reason owners switch to filtered water isn't safety — it's palatability. Chlorine and other treatment chemicals can affect taste and smell in ways that put some cats off drinking, and a basic carbon filter (like the ones built into most cat fountains) removes a lot of that.

If you're on well water, or you have any specific concern about your local water quality, that's worth checking against your municipal water report or a vet's advice rather than guessing — filtering is about taste for most households, not a substitute for knowing your water source is safe.

How often should I change the water in a fountain?

As a general rule: a full water change and basin rinse every 3–4 days, with a proper deep clean (basin, pump, and impeller) every 1–2 weeks to keep biofilm and mineral buildup from forming — that buildup is exactly the kind of thing that makes water taste "off" to a cat and defeats the purpose of the fountain.

Filter cartridges are separate from the water itself and typically need replacing every 2–4 weeks depending on the model and how many cats share it — check your specific fountain's manual, since this varies more by brand than the water-change schedule does.

Do ice cubes in the water bowl actually help?

Some cats do find them more interesting — a bobbing ice cube can turn the bowl into something worth investigating, and it works especially well when the cube is made from a bit of low-sodium broth rather than plain water (see the hydration tips above). It's not a hydration fix on its own, more a novelty that occasionally nudges a reluctant drinker over.

It's not necessary for most cats, and there's no need to force it if yours isn't interested — a wide, clean bowl in the right spot does more heavy lifting than ice ever will.

I just bought a wider bowl and my cat won't touch it — now what?

Totally normal. Cats can be wary of anything new in their space, bowls included. Try placing the new bowl right next to the old one for a few days rather than swapping it outright, and keep both filled — most cats will start alternating and eventually favor the wider one once it stops feeling unfamiliar.

If it's been more than a week or two with zero interest, double-check placement and material against the whisker fatigue checklist above — sometimes it's less about "new" and more about something else in the setup still not being quite right.

Does my cat's water intake normally change with the seasons?

Yes, mild seasonal shifts are expected — more drinking in hot weather or after extra activity, less on cooler, low-activity days. That kind of gradual, weather-linked change isn't usually a concern.

What's worth paying attention to is a change that's sudden, large, or doesn't track with anything obvious like weather or a new food. That pattern is different from normal seasonal drift, and it's exactly what the health conditions section above and the tracker are meant to help you catch early.

How much water does my cat need if they eat only kibble vs. wet food?

The gap is bigger than most owners expect. Dry food is only around 10% moisture, so a cat eating kibble exclusively needs to get nearly all of its water from the bowl — often close to 90% of its total daily need. Wet food, by contrast, is typically 75–80% moisture, so a cat on an all-wet diet may only need to actively drink a small fraction of its total water needs, since most of it is already coming from the food itself.

Rather than a single number, the exact target depends on your cat's weight and age too — that's exactly what the calculator above works out for you, including a side-by-side breakdown of how much should come from the bowl versus how much wet food would cover the same amount.

Why isn't my cat drinking water?

Usually it comes down to one of a handful of physical, fixable causes rather than the cat being "stubborn." The most common: a bowl that's too narrow or deep (see the whisker fatigue check above), water placed too close to food or the litter box, standing water that's gone stale or picked up an odor, or simply a diet that's already wet-food-heavy enough that the cat doesn't feel the need to drink much at the bowl at all.

The hydration tips section above covers the fixes for most of these. If you've ruled those out and your cat still isn't drinking, or you're also noticing any of the signs of dehydration, that's the point to loop in a vet rather than keep troubleshooting at home.

How do I calculate total fluid intake including canned food?

The short version: total fluid intake is drinking water plus the water already inside whatever your cat eats. Canned/wet food is typically about 75–80% moisture by weight, so a 3 oz can contributes roughly 2.2–2.4 oz of that as water — dry kibble, by comparison, contributes very little (~10% moisture).

You don't need to do this math by hand — enter your cat's weight, age, and diet into the calculator above, and it splits the total daily target into exactly this: how much is already covered by food moisture, and how much still needs to come from the bowl. The dual-result panel below your result even converts that bowl amount into an equivalent wet-food portion, if you'd rather think in cans than ounces.
